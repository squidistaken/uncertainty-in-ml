\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset}

The dataset consists of daily S\&P~500 closing prices from 1993 to 2024, obtained from Kaggle \parencite{sp500}. It captures multiple market regimes, including periods of economic expansion, financial crises, and elevated volatility, making it well-suited for evaluating both predictive performance and uncertainty estimation in non-stationary financial environments.

Since raw asset prices are non-stationary (the mean and variance change over time, violating assumptions of probabilistic models such as Gaussian Processes), the closing prices are transformed into dividend-adjusted log returns as equation \ref{eq:log_return}:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \log\ \left(\frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}}\right),
    \label{eq:log_return}
\end{equation}
where $P_t$ is the closing price and $D_t$ is any dividend paid on day $t$. Log returns are more stationary and represent relative rather than absolute price changes. They are then standardised to zero mean and unit variance, with the scaling parameters fitted exclusively on the training split and applied to all splits, preventing any leakage of future distributional information. The standardised log return is denoted $\tilde{r}_t$.

Beyond the standardised return, three additional features are engineered at each time step: the absolute return $|\tilde{r}_t|$ as an approximation for daily realised volatility, a five-day rolling standard deviation $\sigma_5^{(t)}$ to capture short-term volatility clustering, and a 21-day rolling standard deviation $\sigma_{21}^{(t)}$ to capture medium-term volatility regime. Rows with insufficient history for the rolling statistics are discarded.

A sliding window of 21 trading days (approximately one calendar month of trading) is then applied to form input-output pairs: each input $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21 \times 4}$ contains all four features over the preceding 21 days, and the target $y_t = \tilde{r}_{t+1}$ is the standardised log return on the following day. The data is split chronologically: training (1993-2016), validation (2017-2020), and test (2021-2024). Randomly shuffling would introduce look-ahead bias and violate the natural temporal ordering; this split ensures models are evaluated in a realistic out-of-sample forecasting setting. 

The training period additionally spans several high-volatility episodes, including the dot-com crash (2000-2002), the global financial crisis (2007-2009), and the European debt crisis (2011-2012). Exposure to these sharp return spikes during training encourages both models to learn not only typical low-volatility behaviour but also the more extreme regimes that are of greatest interest for risk quantification.
